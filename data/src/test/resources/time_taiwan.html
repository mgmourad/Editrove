The southern Taiwanese city of Chiayi has long been synonymous with a cherished culinary staple: turkey rice, served drenched in savory soy broth and topped with lard-fried shallots and pickled radish. Introduced by American GIs after World War II, the non-native bird became an affordable alternative to chicken and quickly became a local icon. 

Eight decades later, Chiayi is anchoring its identity in a very different military legacy: uncrewed aerial vehicles (UAVs). Today, more than 50 companies and research institutions have set up shop nearby, transforming the county into Taiwan’s premier drone hub.

Taiwan's drone industry gained newfound urgency following Vladimir Putin’s February 2022 invasion of Ukraine, where both sides have relied heavily on commercial drones—most notably those produced by Chinese manufacturer DJI. But when reports emerged that DJI’s AeroScope system could track the real-time positions of drone operators, cybersecurity concerns exploded. With Beijing and Moscow bound by a "no-limits" partnership, Taiwan has sought to meet growing demand for "non-Red" supply chains.

Yet drones form just one part of Taiwan’s broader push to ground its security in technological supremacy. By embedding itself irrevocably into global technology supply chains, Taiwan seeks to insulate its 23 million citizens against Beijing’s looming threat of invasion. This industrial dominance has long been dubbed a “silicon shield,” raising the global economic cost of a cross-Strait conflict to catastrophic levels. 

Advertisement

As geopolitical pressure from Beijing intensifies, Taiwan is weaponizing this commercial electronics ecosystem into an asymmetric shield. The goal is to turn the island's commercial manufacturing grid into an arsenal of low-cost, networked security nodes, rather than attempting to match China ship for ship or jet for jet. As such, Taiwan is quietly redefining 21st-century national defense, fusing advanced semiconductors, proprietary software, and autonomous drone swarms. 

Chiayi-based Kunway Technology began by manufacturing agricultural crop-spraying drones over a decade ago, but pivoted to defense aircraft in the wake of the war in Ukraine. Today, Kunway ships more than 2,000 UAVs to Kyiv each month. “We need to prepare, because Taiwan is isolated,” David Liu, CEO of Kunway, tells TIME. “If war happens, we need to produce military drones by ourselves.”

At the firm’s headquarters—housed within the former Chiayi campus of the National Taiwan University of Sport—technicians test an array of hardware, which ranges from “kamikaze” quadcopters and spring-launched surveillance drones to stealth unmanned surface vessels.

After taking office in May 2024, President Lai Ching-te designated drones as one of Taiwan’s five “trustworthy industries” aimed at deepening ties with democratic allies. Taipei has since signed memorandums of understanding with partners including France, Poland, the Czech Republic, and the U.S., all seeking alternatives to supply chains rooted in China, which controls 70% to 80% of global drone production.

“You still need to build up this kind of international collaboration among all these democratic countries,” says Puma Shen, a lawmaker for the ruling Democratic Progressive Party (DPP), who visited Ukraine in 2023. “Because we need to fight together.”

To sharpen its technological edge, Taiwan’s state-run National Chung-Shan Institute of Science and Technology (NCSIST) has partnered with nine foreign software firms—including defense-tech heavyweights Anduril, Auterion, and Shield AI—focusing on autonomous flight, AI command-and-control, and swarm intelligence. The strategy is delivering dramatic results: Taiwan’s drone exports exploded from just 3,472 units in 2024 to 122,726 in 2025. In the first seven months of 2026 alone, another 210,000 units worth $238 million took flight for foreign markets.

But drones are just a small part of a complex web of technological dependence that Taipei hopes will help secure its precious autonomy. Today, Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co. (TSMC) produces more than 90% of the world's most advanced chips, while Taiwanese manufacturers assemble 90% of global AI servers, according to Taiwan’s Market Intelligence and Consulting Institute, and hold a virtual monopoly on vital data-center infrastructure, such as high-efficiency power units and liquid-cooling thermal systems. Every titan of modern computing—from Apple and Nvidia to AMD and Qualcomm—has its supply chain entwined with the island. Propelled by this indispensable role in the AI revolution, in October Taiwan’s stock exchange briefly surged to become the world's fourth-largest by market value.

“If you look at the AI accelerators powering data centers today from all the existing incumbents, they're all TSMC-manufactured,” says Qualcomm CEO Cristiano Amon. “The entire semiconductor industry is used to that, and I think we want it to continue that way.”

That global importance was on full display at COMPUTEX 2026. Under the banner "AI Together," the trade fair transformed Taipei into an electrifying carnival of modern computing. A record-setting 111,312 buyers and industry leaders from 152 countries filled the halls, navigating a sprawling grid of 1,500 exhibitors across 6,000 booths featuring breakthroughs in physical AI, robotics, and next-generation silicon.

In late May, chip giant Nvidia broke ground on a $1.3 billion global headquarters in northern Taipei, built to accommodate more than 10,000 engineers and researchers. Taipei Mayor Chiang Wan-an attended the ceremony, underscoring how preserving and expanding the island’s tech supremacy remains a rare point of cross-party consensus in Taiwanese politics.

“It's not just about the company and the technology, it's more about the talent, the people,” says Shen, who is the DPP’s candidate for Taipei Mayor in Nov. 28 elections. “You also need [to tell] all the companies of the world that, ‘hey, Taipei, right now is ready.’”

Yet Taiwan’s pivot toward defense applications has been controversial. Opposition lawmakers have repeatedly stalled the Lai administration’s proposed $40 billion special defense budget, ultimately passing a scaled-back $25 billion package. While that funding authorized two separate U.S. arms purchases, it stripped out investments meant to bolster Taiwan’s domestic defense industry—clouding the future of indigenous initiatives like the Strong Bow anti-ballistic missile, intended as the backbone of Taiwan’s prospective "T-Dome" air defense system.

Opposition leaders maintain their resistance stems from a lack of transparency regarding how the funds would be allocated, while the DPP accuses rivals of undermining national security to Beijing's benefit. Domestic drone manufacturers worry their nascent sector could lose momentum if the opposition Nationalists (KMT) reclaims the presidency in 2028.

Advertisement

“We are not opposed to building national defense,” says Cheng Li-wun, chairperson of the opposition KMT. “The problem is that the proposal lacks substance—it's blank. Given the DPP's reputation for corruption, we are unwilling to authorize it without oversight.”

Beyond political bickering, commercial realities hobble Taiwan’s defense hardware makers—chiefly cost. Taiwanese-built drones cost roughly twice as much as Chinese equivalents. Supply chain bottlenecks present another hurdle: Kunway routinely exports drones to Ukraine without batteries, which Kyiv purchases separately from China. 

To maintain hardware compatibility, Kunway must still import connector cables from Chinese suppliers. The hope is that, as Taiwan’s defense industry develops, costs can come down and non-Red supply chains mature. Yet fears remain that the very prowess that Taiwan is basing its security on also makes the island an even more tantalizing prize—one that, if seized, would hand Beijing a commanding lead over transformative technologies. 

Advertisement

Despite these frictions, tech executives are bullish that the island’s core strategic value remains unassailable. KS Pua, the Malaysian-born CEO of Taiwanese memory giant Phison, insists the island’s “silicon shield” remains as robust as ever. 

“I can use a foreigner's voice to tell everyone Taiwan is very safe,” he says. “Don't worry, there are some arguments, some noise, but nothing will change. The world needs Taiwan.”
